# Who Profits From Your Work?

*A modern adaptation of Karl Marx's* Wage Labour and Capital *(1849) and Friedrich Engels's introduction to the 1891 edition. Not a translation: the faithful translation, verse by verse, is in `works/wage-labour-and-capital/`. Citations like `WLC I.13.1` point to the verse in the original.*

## Outline

### 1. You sell your time

- What the reader learns: a wage is the price of your ability to work, bought by the hour or the piece.
- Draws on: `WLC I.8.1` (ask workers what they earn), `WLC I.13.1` (labor-power is a commodity), `WLC I.13.2`, `WLC I.13.3` (why sell it? to live), `WLC I.14.6`, `WLC I.15.1`, `WLC I.15.5`, `WLC I.15.7` (the serf, the slave and the free worker: you can quit a boss but not the boss class).
- Modern example: a $20-an-hour warehouse worker, a gig driver paid by the delivery.

### 2. Your work creates value

- What the reader learns: everything a business owns started as somebody's work; your shift adds new value to the materials.
- Draws on: `WLC III.1.2` (capital is accumulated labor), `WLC III.5.1` (people produce together, not alone), `WLC III.17.7` (the worker hands over the power to create value).
- Modern example: what a shift of picking and packing adds to what the warehouse sells.

### 3. Where does the profit come from?

- What the reader learns: you cover your own wage in part of the day and work the rest for the owner. This is Engels's worked example, updated.
- Draws on: `WLC P1891.11.1`, `WLC P1891.11.2` (a daily wage for a 12-hour day), `WLC P1891.19.1` to `WLC P1891.19.9` (six hours pays the wage), `WLC P1891.20.1` to `WLC P1891.20.5` ("Hold on there!": the other six hours belong to the boss).
- Modern example: one worker's paycheck set against the revenue per employee of a large retailer. Use real, sourced numbers and say where they come from.
- Beyond the original: Engels's example uses 1891 money and a 12-hour day. Say plainly that the numbers are ours and the logic is his.

### 4. Why can't you just ask for more?

- What the reader learns: wages rise and fall with competition among workers and with what it costs to keep a worker alive; even when wages rise, the owners' share can rise faster.
- Draws on: `WLC II.10.6` (cost of production), `WLC IV.11.1`, `WLC IV.12.1` (real and relative wages), `WLC IV.14.8` (the owners' power grows), `WLC V.20.4` (the more you work, the less you get).
- Modern example: wages against productivity since the 1970s. Sourced figures only.

### 5. What happens when business grows?

- What the reader learns: "what's good for the company is good for the workers" is half true at best; growth brings machinery, deskilling and crises.
- Draws on: `WLC IV.24.1` (the worker's "interest" in capital's growth), `WLC V.33.4` (crises and the hecatombs of workers).
- Modern example: automation in warehouses and call centers; layoffs after record profits.

### 6. What can workers do?

- What the reader learns: what workers have done together, from unions to strikes to cooperatives.
- Beyond the original: Marx planned this part (`WLC I.4.1`) but the series broke off before it (`WLC P1891.1.3`). Engels closes his 1891 introduction with the claim that a new social order is possible and that workers are organizing for it (`WLC P1891.24.5` to `WLC P1891.24.8`). Everything else in this chapter is ours: label it, and point readers to the Manifesto (`CM II.65.1` to `CM II.81.1`) and to the organizing works in the library as they are added. Present the anarchist, syndicalist and party approaches side by side.

## Questions for discussion

- Who owns your workplace?
- Who decides your wages?
- What happens if everyone refuses to work?
- Who benefits when productivity increases?
- What would change if workers controlled production?
