# Cruxes in The Condition of the Working Class in England

Good first Discussions. Title the thread with the citation, for example `[CWC V.2.4] social murder`.

| Verse | Problem |
|---|---|
| `P1892.3.3` | Engels's English preface takes "the Polish Jew" as the type of petty, cheating trade, "Jew or Christian" in the same sentence notwithstanding. A stereotype of the time, in his own English. Translate it as written and say plainly in a note what it is; do not soften it in the modern draft and do not build on it. |
| `P1892.19.6` to `P1892.28.5` | The "aristocracy among the working-class" and the claim that the English workers "shared in the benefits" of England's industrial monopoly (`P1892.28.1`). Later writers built a theory of the labour aristocracy and of imperialism on these lines. Neutral wording here; the readings belong in `commentary/`. |
| `P1892.30.3` to `P1892.31.3` | The "New Unionism" of the East End, unions of the unskilled led by socialists. Names and events of 1889 to 1891 need short notes. |
| `D.0.1` to `D.6.1` | The dedication was written in English; only MEW's German translation is here. The modern draft should follow Engels's English once it is sourced, not MEW's German. |
| `E.1.1` | "Die Geschichte der arbeitenden Klasse" comes out in Wischnewetzky as "the history of the proletariat". Engels's words for the working class carry weight in this book; see the glossary entry. |
| `II.3.1`, `II.3.2`, `III.2.1` | The "war of all against all" (Hobbes) as the truth of bourgeois society, with a jab at "Freund Stirner" (`II.3.2`). One wording throughout; a note can name Hobbes and Stirner. |
| `II.5.8`, `V.2.1` to `V.2.4`, `V.17.8` | "Sozialer Mord": Engels argues like a prosecutor that society knowingly kills workers. The legal words (Mord, Totschlag) must stay exact. |
| `III.11.9` to `III.11.16` | The crisis cycle, here "every five years"; Marx and Engels later spoke of ten. Translate his figure; a note can date it. |
| `III.12.1` to `III.12.5` | "Reserve von Arbeitern". Wischnewetzky writes "reserve army", the term Marx made famous later; Engels in 1845 says only "Reserve". |
| `IV.3.1` to `IV.4.22` | Carlyle's and Engels's descriptions of Irish immigrants (dirt, drink, pigs in the house, "but little above the savage"). Engels blames English rule and poverty and calls Carlyle one-sided (`IV.4.1`), yet repeats much of the picture. Period stereotypes about a people: translate faithfully and say so in a note. |
| `V.48.1` to `V.49.1` | Drink and sexual "licence" among workers, and the faults of the working class summed up as want of self-restraint. Engels puts the cause on their conditions. Plain, sober wording; no added colour. |
| `VI.10.2` to `VI.12.2` | Women as breadwinners and men at home: "der den Mann entmannt und dem Weibe seine Weiblichkeit nimmt" (`VI.12.2`). Engels's gender language of 1845; keep it as his and leave the arguments to `commentary/`. |
| `VI.11.1` to `VI.11.4` | The weaver's letter, which Engels prints in the writer's own misspelled German and Wischnewetzky re-translates into Lancashire English (her note {145}). The modern draft should be plain English; a note can say how the original reads. The truck-system letter in `VI.74` is the same case. |
| `VI.18.1` | "Jus primae noctis": sexual coercion of women workers by their employers. Translate exactly. |
| `VI.77.1` to `VI.86.1` | Edward P. Mead's poem "The Steam King" (1843), which Engels translated into German. The English editions leave it out. Mead's English original is public domain; when it is found it should fill the English column, and the modern draft should follow it. |
| `VIII.26.8`, `XI.29.4`, `XI.29.6` | Engels forecasts the next crisis for 1846 or 1847 and revolution soon after. His notes of 1887 and 1892 say "And it came in 1847" and "Ist pünktlich eingetroffen" (`III.N4`, `VIII.N6`). Keep the forecasts as forecasts; the notes say what happened. |
| `VIII.29.6` | "Dixi et salvavi animam meam" (I have spoken and saved my soul), Latin with MEW's German gloss. Keep the Latin with a translation. |
| `X.18.4`, `X.N5` | Engels's note of 1892 says plainly "Irrtum" (mistake) about his account of small farming in Ireland. A good place to show readers how Engels corrected himself. |
| `XI.2.1` | "I have never seen a class so deeply demoralised ... as the English bourgeoisie." Polemic; translate at full strength. |
| `XI.29.9` to `XI.29.24` | War of the poor against the rich, a new Gironde (`XI.29.10`), a "Ninth Thermidor" (`XI.29.23`), and Communism standing "above the breach between bourgeoisie and proletariat" (`XI.29.17`). Engels's position in 1845, before the Manifesto. Readers of every tendency quote it for their own ends; neutral wording, readings in `commentary/`. |

## Alignment notes

Wischnewetzky splits Engels's long sentences at semicolons, so most review flags are `1:2` (315 of 528). Her English quotations from British authors are often the originals Engels translated from (Alison in `V.36`, Ure in `VIII.13`), so wording and length differ; check that each English group covers the German verse. The ingest regrouped eight runs where the English slipped; see `REALIGN` in SOURCES.md.
