# Cruxes in Anarchism and Other Essays

Good first Discussions. Title the thread with the citation, for example `[AOE I.14.1] Goldman's definition of anarchism`.

The essays are English originals, so most cruxes are about what a modern reader will hear in 1910 words, and about passages that need a note rather than a quiet rewrite.

| Verse | Question |
|---|---|
| `I.14.1` | Goldman's definition: "the philosophy of a new social order based on liberty unrestricted by man-made law". The rest of essay I takes it apart phrase by phrase, so the draft should change it as little as it can. |
| `I.26.5` and `I.46.1` to `I.46.2` | The positive programme: voluntary associations "gradually developing into free communism", and the threefold liberation set out in `I.46.1`. These are the verses readers quote. Keep the parallel structure of `I.46.1`. |
| `I.2.1` to `I.3.1` | John Henry Mackay's poem "Anarchy", quoted in an unnamed English version. The verses follow its sentences, so some break mid-line (`I.2.3`, `I.2.4`). Decide whether the draft renders the poem as verse in one piece, with the other verse ids joined to the first, or keeps it sentence by sentence. Whoever made this English version should be identified in a note. |
| `II.1.1` to `II.23.1` | Minorities Versus Majorities. Goldman expected this essay to be called anti-democratic (`P1910.12.2`) and it still is. She is against the crowd as a political force, not against working people (`II.22.1`). The draft must keep the sharpness without adding contempt she did not write. `II.22.7` to `II.22.11` are Emerson, not Goldman; keep the quotation marks visible. |
| `III.1.1` to `III.85.8` | The Psychology of Political Violence. Goldman explains acts of violence by the conditions that drove them; she does not call for them. The draft has to keep that line exactly where she draws it, neither turning explanation into endorsement nor into apology. "Attentater" needs a settled rendering first (see the glossary). |
| `III.26.2` to `III.27.1` | "lulled him to sleep with, / My country, 'tis of thee". The quotation finishes Goldman's sentence. The verse break falls inside the sentence because the book sets the anthem as a block. |
| `III.49.2` to `III.50.1` | "was engraved, significantly, VAILLANT!" Same pattern: the engraved word is its own verse because the book centres it on a line. |
| `III.83.3` and `III.N4` | "Madar Sol Dhingra" is Madan Lal Dhingra, who shot Sir Curzon Wyllie in London in 1909. Keep Goldman's spelling in the historical column; the draft can use the correct name with a note. |
| `IV.15.1` to `IV.16.1` | Wilde's Ballad of Reading Gaol, quoted as one stanza. The draft quotes Wilde, it does not modernise him. |
| `V.3.2` | "Patriotism, sir, is the last resort of scoundrels," said Dr. Johnson. Boswell gives it as "the last refuge of a scoundrel". Goldman's wording stays in the historical column; the draft may use either form, with a note. |
| `V.10.1` to `V.14.3` | Military spending figures of 1901 to 1910. Keep them as hers; any comparison with today belongs in commentary. |
| `VI.45.1` to `VI.49.1` | Ferrer's last letter from prison, a translation of unknown hand from the French in L'Humanité. Draft it as a letter, keeping the dateline and the signature as separate verses. |
| `VII.6.7` | "the Puritanism bred in the Anglo-Saxon blood". A racial idiom of the period for a cultural tradition. Translate what she says and mark the idiom in a note; do not silently turn it into "culture". |
| `VIII.1.1` to `VIII.55.5` | The Traffic in Women. Goldman's thesis is that prostitution has economic causes (`VIII.9.3`, `VIII.16.1`) and that the white slave panic hides them. Her terms are the reformers' terms used against them (see the glossary). The draft keeps her irony; scare quotes in the original must survive. |
| `VIII.47.2` to `VIII.47.11` | Goldman rejects the claim that Jewish girls are the main victims of trafficking, but argues it from "Jewish psychology" and the claim that "the Jewish girl is not adventurous". The aim is to refute an antisemitic myth; the method is a group generalisation. Keep both and add a note. |
| `VIII.30.1` and `VIII.N5` | "Banger" is the Dutch criminologist Willem Bonger (Criminalité et conditions économiques, 1905). The book spells the name the same wrong way twice, so it is kept as printed; the draft and a note can give Bonger. |
| `IX.21.1` and `IX.23.5` | "Woman, essentially a purist, is naturally bigotted". Goldman generalises about women in an essay against women's suffrage as a cure-all. Readers will meet these lines first; the draft keeps them and lets the essay's argument (the vote does not free anyone, `IX.33.4`) stand beside them. |
| `B.9.1` and `B.35.4` | Havel writes of "the Jewish race" and its traits, meaning praise. The race language of 1910 needs a note in either direction, praise or blame. |
| `B.51.1` to `B.51.11` | McKinley's assassination and Czolgosz. Havel's account of the press campaign against Goldman. Dates and names should be checked in a note. |
| `XI.4.1` and `XI.26.1` to `XI.27.1` | "Marriage is primarily an economic arrangement, an insurance pact", and the answer "Free love?" A modern reader hears "free love" as casual sex; Goldman means love without legal or economic compulsion. The draft keeps her words and lets `XI.26.1` define them. |
| `XII.1.1` to `XII.98.1` | The Modern Drama. Most of the essay is plot summary of plays by Ibsen, Hauptmann, Shaw and others, with play titles in capitals and footnotes giving their English names. Decide how the draft sets titles (italics) and whether to give the English title or the original. |

## Capitals in the text

The transcription marks italics with capitals, for titles and foreign words (`THE HOUSE OF BONDAGE`, `VIII.8.1`; `ATTENTATER`) and for stressed words (`the LIVING John Browns`, `B.3.7`; `HIS country`, `V.6.2`). The historical column keeps them; the draft does not shout. Use italics where the reader supports them, or plain text.
