# Cruxes in The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte

Good first Discussions. Title the thread with the citation, for example `[EB VII.15.12] they cannot represent themselves`.

| Verse | Problem |
|---|---|
| `I.1.1` to `I.1.2` | Tragedy and farce. Hegel says that great events repeat; the tragedy and the farce are Marx's own addition (Engels had used the image in a letter to Marx of 3 December 1851). The Gutenberg English reads "that that great historic facts", probably a slip for "all"; check the 1898 printing before relying on it. |
| `I.2.1` | "Die Menschen machen ihre eigene Geschichte" is one of the most quoted sentences in the library. De Leon's "out of the whole cloth" is his own image. Settle a plain, exact wording here first. |
| `I.8.2` | "Hic Rhodus, hic salta! Hier ist die Rose, hier tanze!" The Latin is from Aesop by way of Hegel's preface to the *Philosophy of Right*, and the German plays on Rhodus/Rhodos and rhodon (rose). A note, not a paraphrase. |
| `IV.13.5`, `V.4.6` to `V.4.9` | The lumpenproletariat. V.4.7 is Marx's list of its members (vagabonds, dismissed soldiers, pickpockets, brothel keepers ...). Translate the list as written. Whether the term is analysis or contempt is a question for commentary; see the glossary. |
| `VII.10.1` to `VII.12.2` | The state machinery. VII.10.8 says every revolution perfected the machine instead of breaking it. Anarchist and Marxist traditions read this, and CWF III.3.1, very differently; keep the wording neutral and the readings in `commentary/`. |
| `VII.27.5` | The variant: in 1852 the paragraph went on with the smashing of the state machinery and the proletarian revolution's "chorus" among the peasants. Marx cut it in 1869. Later writers (Lenin among them) quote the 1852 lines. Show the variant to readers; do not fold it into the modern draft. |
| `VII.15.8` to `VII.15.13` | The sack of potatoes and "Sie können sich nicht vertreten, sie müssen vertreten werden". The whole passage on when peasants do and do not form a class turns on two "insofern" sentences (VII.15.9, VII.15.10) that De Leon runs together. |
| `VII.9.7` | "Brav gewühlt, alter Maulwurf!" quotes Hamlet (I.5) by way of Hegel. De Leon's "Well hast thou grubbed, old mole!" keeps the quotation; a modern draft should too. |
| `VII.11.1` | The Gutenberg English is garbled ("under the absolute monarchy, was only the means whereby the first revolution"). Translate from the German; do not try to repair De Leon. |
| `III.5.1`, `V.2.19` | De Leon's slips: December 20, 1849 for 1848, and a Permanent Committee of 18 for 28. The German is right; note the English. |
| `VII.33.11` | Soulouque, the Emperor of Haiti, as a figure of fun. Marx's comparison, and De Leon's 1898 note on it, carry the racial attitudes of their time. Translate Marx exactly and say so in a note. |
| `P1869.8.1` | Marx rejects "Caesarism" as a schoolbook analogy, because Rome's class struggle was fought inside a privileged minority. The modern draft should keep the argument, not just the conclusion. |
| `P1885.4.2` | Engels states the "great law of motion of history". The sentence is long and builds in stages; resist splitting it into slogans. |
| `VII.34.3` | The closing prophecy about the Vendôme column, which Marx says in `P1869.6.1` had come true. A note can say how. |

## Alignment notes

De Leon joins Marx's short sentences with semicolons and dashes, so most review flags are `2:1` and `3:1` matches. Check that each English group covers exactly the German verses joined to it.
